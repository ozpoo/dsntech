AI as a Design Thinking Partner: Faculty Classroom Activities

Introduction

This document provides a collection of classroom activities designed to help students engage with artificial intelligence as a design thinking partner rather than a replacement for creative practice.

The goal of these activities is not to teach students how to generate finished designs with AI. Instead, they use AI as a tool for expanding the traditional design process: developing empathy, strengthening critique, exploring possibilities, and understanding how design systems function.

Each activity connects to a larger philosophy:

Design is not simply the creation of artifacts — it is the shaping of relationships, experiences, and behaviors.

Professional designers do not only make things. They investigate audiences, evaluate communication, create systems, and make intentional decisions based on context. These activities help students practice those same habits while developing a more critical understanding of where AI can—and cannot—support the design process.

How Faculty Can Use This Document

These activities are designed to be flexible and adaptable across courses, disciplines, and levels of experience. They can be used as:

  • Short classroom exercises (10–30 minutes)
  • Pre-critique preparation activities
  • Project development checkpoints
  • Reflection exercises
  • Studio discussion prompts
  • Assignments integrated into existing projects

Faculty do not need to redesign an entire course to incorporate these activities. Each exercise can be inserted into an existing design workflow at a moment where students would already be asking similar questions.

For example:

  • Use Instant Audience Lab early in a project when students are defining goals, users, and communication strategies.
  • Use AI as a Critique Partner before a studio critique to help students arrive prepared with questions and revision ideas.
  • Use System Generator during concept development when students need to move beyond individual solutions toward scalable design systems.

A Different Model for AI in Design Education

Many conversations about AI in design focus on speed, automation, or production output. These activities intentionally shift that conversation.

Rather than asking:

“How can AI make the artifact faster?”

Students are encouraged to ask:

“How can AI help me think more deeply about the problem?”

This approach positions AI as:

  • A research assistant that helps uncover perspectives
  • A critique partner that challenges assumptions
  • A systems collaborator that helps explore relationships and variation

The designer remains responsible for interpretation, judgment, authorship, and decision-making.

The Progression

Together, these activities follow a progression that mirrors professional design practice:

1. Audience

Who is it for?

Students develop empathy and learn to evaluate whether their design communicates effectively to specific people.

2. Critique

Is it working?

Students practice evaluating design from multiple perspectives and identifying opportunities for refinement.

3. System

How does it scale?

Students move beyond individual artifacts and begin designing rules, relationships, and frameworks that can generate multiple outcomes.

Implementation Notes

These activities work best when AI outputs are treated as starting points for discussion, not authoritative answers. Encourage students to question AI-generated responses:

  • What assumptions is this making?
  • What perspectives are missing?
  • What information is inaccurate or oversimplified?
  • What decisions still require human judgment?

The purpose is not to reduce the designer’s role, but to strengthen the designer’s ability to think critically, communicate intentionally, and make informed decisions in an increasingly complex creative environment.

I’d probably put this page before the three activities as the faculty “why” section. It gives skeptical faculty a framework first, then the exercises feel like practical extensions of existing studio pedagogy rather than “here are some AI tricks.”

Activity 1: Instant Audience Lab

Design Begins with People

Core Principle

Design is communication. Before we can evaluate whether something is visually successful, we have to understand who it is speaking to. Audience is often discussed in broad, abstract terms—“design for your users”—without giving students practical ways to imagine those users.

This activity uses AI to rapidly generate believable audience perspectives that students can use to test assumptions, identify blind spots, and strengthen design decisions.

Rather than asking AI to design, students ask it to help them better understand the people they are designing for.

Learning Outcomes

After completing this activity, students should be able to:

  • Translate abstract audiences into concrete user profiles.
  • Identify how different audiences interpret the same visual communication.
  • Recognize mismatches between design intention and audience perception.
  • Make evidence-informed revisions based on audience feedback.

Faculty Introduction

“One of the biggest challenges for beginning designers is that they often design for themselves. Today we’re going to use AI to quickly generate several audience perspectives so we can test whether our work communicates the way we intend.”

Student Prompt

I am designing a [project type] about [topic].

Generate three detailed audience personas.

For each persona include:

  • age
  • occupation
  • background
  • motivations
  • goals
  • visual preferences
  • prior knowledge
  • accessibility considerations
  • what they might misunderstand

Then explain how each audience would interpret my design.

What would resonate?

What would confuse them?

What questions would they still have?

Reflection Prompt

After reviewing the responses, ask:

  • Which audience surprised you?
  • Which audience understood your design the least?
  • What assumptions did you make that weren’t shared by your audience?
  • What is one revision you would make based on this feedback?

Deliverable (10–15 minutes)

Students submit:

  • Three audience personas
  • Three observations for each persona
  • One design revision they plan to make
  • A short paragraph explaining why that revision improves communication

Why This Works

Instead of asking students to “think about their audience,” this activity gives them multiple concrete viewpoints in minutes. It encourages empathy, improves critique discussions, and helps students recognize that design success depends on communication—not personal preference.

Activity 2: AI as a Critique Partner

Critique as Conversation

Core Principle

Critique is not about determining whether a design is “good” or “bad.” It is an ongoing conversation that reveals strengths, weaknesses, competing priorities, and opportunities for refinement.

AI can support this process by simulating multiple informed perspectives before students enter the classroom. The goal is not to replace peer critique or instructor feedback, but to help students arrive better prepared for meaningful discussion.

Learning Outcomes

Students will:

  • Practice receiving constructive criticism.
  • Compare conflicting viewpoints.
  • Identify recurring patterns across critiques.
  • Develop intentional revision strategies.

Faculty Introduction

“Professional designers rarely receive a single opinion. They receive feedback from clients, users, creative directors, engineers, accessibility experts, marketers, and many others. Today we’ll use AI to simulate those conversations before our in-class critique.”

Student Prompt

Act as an experienced Art Director reviewing this design.

Provide honest feedback focused on:

  • clarity
  • hierarchy
  • concept
  • composition
  • typography
  • audience

Explain both strengths and weaknesses.

Then:

Now review the same design as an Accessibility Specialist.

Then:

Review it again as the intended audience.

Finally:

Compare these critiques.

Where do they agree?

Where do they disagree?

What tensions exist between these perspectives?

What revisions would best satisfy the greatest number of concerns?

Reflection Prompt

Students answer:

  • Which critique felt most valuable?
  • Which perspectives conflicted?
  • Which suggestions aligned with your own thinking?
  • Which suggestions will you ignore—and why?

Deliverable (15–20 minutes)

Students submit:

  • Three critique perspectives
  • One summary of recurring themes
  • One summary of conflicting viewpoints
  • Two planned revisions with justification

Why This Works

Instead of replacing critique, AI helps students practice critique before class begins. Students become more reflective, more articulate, and more prepared to participate in meaningful discussion with peers and instructors.

Activity 3: System Generator

Design Systems Instead of Single Solutions

Core Principle

Strong designers do not simply create isolated artifacts—they build systems capable of producing many coherent outcomes.

Rather than asking AI to generate finished designs, students use it to help define the rules, variables, and constraints that make visual systems scalable and adaptable.

This shifts AI away from idea generation and toward systems thinking.

Learning Outcomes

Students will:

  • Understand the relationship between rules and variation.
  • Distinguish between isolated concepts and scalable systems.
  • Develop repeatable visual frameworks.
  • Explore generative approaches to design.

Faculty Introduction

“Instead of asking AI to create a poster, we’re going to ask it to create the rules that could generate hundreds of posters. Designers build systems—not just artifacts.”

Student Prompt

Create a visual design system for a [poster / publication / identity / campaign] about [topic].

Define:

  • color variables
  • typography variables
  • layout rules
  • imagery rules
  • spacing rules
  • hierarchy rules

Explain why each rule exists.

Then generate five distinct design variations that all follow the same system.

Reflection Prompt

Students answer:

  • Which rules remained constant?
  • Which variables changed?
  • Did the designs still feel related?
  • How could this system expand into a larger identity or campaign?

Deliverable (20–30 minutes)

Students submit:

  • One visual system
  • Three to five generated variations
  • A diagram or list of variables and constraints
  • A short explanation of what creates consistency across all outcomes

Why This Works

Students begin thinking like systems designers instead of artifact makers. The exercise naturally connects AI prompting with computational design, generative typography, identity systems, responsive design, and creative coding. It also creates a direct bridge to later work in p5.js, algorithmic design, and responsive web systems.

The Overall Arc

These build on one another as a progression rather than feeling like three disconnected AI activities:

Stage Design Question AI Role Student Learns
1. Audience Who is this for? Research & empathy partner Human-centered design
2. Critique Is it working? Reflective dialogue partner Iteration & evaluation
3. Systems How does it scale? Systems thinking partner Rules, variation & generative design

That sequence reinforces the philosophy outlined: design is not the production of isolated artifacts, but the creation of relationships, behaviors, and systems. It also reframes AI as a tool for inquiry and reflection rather than image generation.
